A wall, built in coats

  1. 01

    Substrate & key

    The wall is cleaned, dampened and given tooth. The first coat is coarse, sandy and deliberately rough, so everything laid over it has something to hold.

    Reads as: open, aggregate-rich, matt

  2. 02

    Scratch coat

    Combed while it is still green. The scoring is not decoration; it is the mechanical key that locks the next coat to this one.

    Reads as: scored, cross-hatched, ridged

  3. 03

    Base coat

    Built out with a straightedge, then floated. Flatness is decided here, well before anyone sees the finish.

    Reads as: fine, sandy, even

  4. 04

    Lime finish

    A thin skin, trowelled and burnished in passes. Every arc of the steel stays legible when raking light crosses the wall.

    Reads as: burnished, cloudy, trowel-marked

The studio

Lime does not hide the hand that laid it. We design for that.

What we make

Hand-applied lime plaster finishes: burnished, trowelled, sand-textured and limewashed surfaces for walls, ceilings, niches, chimney breasts and bar fronts.

Who we work with

Architects and interior designers specifying surfaces for private houses, restaurants and boutique spaces, and the owners who commission them. Based in London.

How it starts

With a conversation about the wall, the light that falls on it and the finish you have in mind. The first step is a material consultation, requested by email.

Process

From a message to a finished wall.

An outline of how a project can run. Stages and sequence are set with you, around the wider build.

Start with a consultation
  1. 01

    Brief

    You send drawings or photographs, the finish you have in mind and a description of the light in the room.

  2. 02

    Samples

    Sample boards let you judge tone, texture and sheen against your own materials and daylight, not a catalogue photograph.

  3. 03

    Specification

    We agree substrate preparation, coats, finish, protection and sequencing with the architect and the wider build team.

  4. 04

    Application

    Each coat is built by hand, wall by wall, and given the time it needs before the next one goes on.

  5. 05

    Cure & care

    Guidance on protecting the surface while it cures and living with it afterwards, including how small repairs are made.

Material story

Four ingredients.
One of them is time.

Lime plaster is old, simple and unforgiving. The simplicity is what makes the finish possible.

  • Fig. A

    Lime putty

    Slaked lime, matured as a putty. It is the binder, and the reason a surface can be worked wet, burnished and repaired later.

  • Fig. B

    Aggregate

    Sand or crushed stone, graded to the finish: coarse for a textured surface, fine for a burnished skin.

  • Fig. C

    Pigment

    Natural earth pigments, when colour is wanted. The colour lives in the body of the plaster rather than on its surface.

  • Fig. D

    Water & time

    Lime hardens by taking carbon dioxide from the air. It cannot be rushed, so the programme has to make room for it.

What it gives a room

  • Vapour-open. Lime walls can exchange moisture with the air, which suits older buildings.
  • Repairable. Damage can be patched and blended rather than replaced.
  • Depth. Polish and pigment read differently through the day, from raking light to lamplight.

Tools of the trade

Steel trowel, hawk, wooden float, scratch comb, straightedge, sponge, burnishing steel, and a lot of clean buckets.
